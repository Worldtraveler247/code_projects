Current Events

Change Healthcare Ransomware Attack

If you've ever been told "we have multi-factor authentication everywhere" and wondered whether that's actually true, this is the incident that proves why you verify it instead of just trusting the policy. In February 2024, one remote-access login screen without that single safeguard turned into the most disruptive healthcare cyberattack in U.S. history, and it's still unwinding more than a year later. Here's how it actually happened.

Change Healthcare processes a huge share of medical claims and prescriptions in the United States -- think of it as the plumbing between pharmacies, hospitals, and insurers. According to the written testimony UnitedHealth Group's then-CEO, Andrew Witty, gave Congress, attackers got in on February 12th using compromised login credentials to reach a Citrix remote-access portal -- a doorway employees used to log into their desktops from outside the office. That portal was supposed to have multi-factor authentication, the second-step login check beyond just a password, turned on. It didn't.

Once inside, the attackers didn't strike right away. They spent roughly nine days moving around the network, and Senate records place the actual theft of protected health information between February 17th and February 20th. Then, on February 21st, they deployed ransomware, encrypting systems and knocking claims-processing offline. Pharmacies across the country couldn't verify insurance. Hospitals couldn't bill. It took weeks to bring systems back, and some smaller providers nearly went under waiting on the cash flow.

Here's the root cause in one sentence: UnitedHealth's own policy required multi-factor authentication on every external-facing system, and one Citrix server that gave direct access to the internal network simply didn't have it -- and nobody caught that gap before someone with a stolen password walked through it. Witty told Congress under oath, quote, "we're trying to dig through exactly why that server had not been protected," meaning even the company that owned the policy couldn't fully explain why enforcement failed at that one point.

What held, and it's worth naming because this incident is mostly a failure story: the ransomware deployment stayed largely contained to the Change Healthcare environment rather than spreading across the entire UnitedHealth Group estate, which points to at least some network segmentation between business units. And the company kept enough logging in place to reconstruct a precise, dated timeline -- the twelfth, the seventeenth through the twentieth, the twenty-first -- which is exactly what let Congress and regulators hold the company accountable afterward.

So what does that mean for you? A senior engineer wouldn't take "we have MFA" at face value. A senior engineer would treat "multi-factor authentication is required on all external-facing systems" as a policy to go verify against an actual inventory, because a stated policy and an enforced control are two different things -- and this breach happened in the gap between them.

If you work anywhere near identity and access management, here's the one-sentence version worth keeping in your back pocket: compromised credentials plus a missing second factor on one internet-facing login was still, in 2024, enough to take down a piece of national healthcare infrastructure.

What Held

  • The ransomware stayed largely scoped to the Change Healthcare environment rather than detonating across UnitedHealth Group's entire IT estate — evidence of at least some segmentation between business units, even though the entry point itself had none of the controls it should have.
  • Logging survived and was detailed enough to reconstruct an exact timeline — the February 12th access date, the February 17th-to-20th exfiltration window, the February 21st ransomware deployment — which is what let the company, Congress, and regulators establish accountability after the fact. A breach with no reconstructable timeline is a far worse interview story to be able to tell.

Senior Security Advice

Enforce Short Session Lifetimes and Lock Down Session Cookies

The Advice

Expire sessions aggressively — short idle timeouts, short absolute lifetimes, and session cookies locked down with the Secure, HttpOnly, and SameSite attributes — so a stolen session token stops being useful fast.

Why It Matters

Once a user logs in, the system hands them a session token so they don't have to re-enter a password on every click. That token is now a bearer credential: whoever holds it is treated as that user, no password required. If a session cookie has no HttpOnly flag, a single cross-site scripting bug lets an attacker's script read it straight out of the browser and reuse it from anywhere. If it has no Secure flag, it can leak over a plain HTTP connection. If it never expires, a token stolen today is still valid next month. This is the mechanism behind session hijacking: the attacker never touches a password, they just replay a live session. It's the same class of weakness OWASP's Session Management Cheat Sheet exists to close, and it's exactly why session binding has its own dedicated section in NIST's digital identity guidelines — a session token is treated as seriously as the login itself.

How to Implement It

  1. Set the session cookie's attributes explicitly: Secure (HTTPS only), HttpOnly (no JavaScript access), and SameSite=Lax or Strict (blocks most cross-site request forgery). Never rely on browser defaults to get this right.
  2. Set both an idle timeout and an absolute timeout. NIST's guidance ties this to assurance level — tighter limits for higher-assurance sessions — but the core idea applies everywhere: a session that's been quiet for a while should die, and even an active session shouldn't live forever.
  3. Invalidate the session server-side on logout, not just client-side. Deleting a cookie in the browser does nothing if the token is still valid on the server.
  4. Rotate the session identifier after any privilege change — most importantly right after login — so a token captured before authentication can't be upgraded into an authenticated one.
  5. Verify it's actually working: log in, pull the session cookie from dev tools, confirm the three attributes are present, then let the session sit idle past the timeout and confirm the next request gets rejected rather than silently renewed.

The Pushback You'll Hear

"Short timeouts just mean people get logged out constantly and complain." That's a real cost, which is why the fix isn't the shortest timeout possible — it's a timeout matched to the sensitivity of what the session can touch, paired with a smooth re-login flow. A ten-minute idle timeout makes sense for an admin console; a line-of-business app can reasonably run longer. What's never acceptable is "no timeout at all," because that turns every future cookie-theft bug into a permanent backdoor instead of a short-lived one.

Tech News

Cisco just disclosed five critical flaws in its Nexus switches that can hand an attacker root access — the highest privilege level there is — with no login required. If your data center runs on that hardware, this is the one to read twice. Two stories tonight: those Cisco Nexus flaws, and a certificate-forgery campaign that let attackers mint real, valid certificates for Google by hijacking three national web domains.

Start with Cisco. According to Cisco's own security advisories and reporting from BleepingComputer and eSecurity Planet, the company disclosed five vulnerabilities on October 7th in NX-OS, the operating system behind Nexus 3000 and Nexus 9000 switches — hardware that anchors a lot of data center and cloud networking. All five carry a near-maximum severity score of nine point eight out of ten. A single crafted request from an unauthenticated attacker can run code with root privileges, or, failing that, crash the process and force the switch to reload. Here's the detail that matters for prioritization: exploitation needs the NX-API feature, or one of two OAM — operations, administration, and maintenance — protocols switched on first, and none of those ship active by default. Cisco says it hasn't seen exploitation in the wild yet. My read: that's exactly the moment to act, before a public proof-of-concept forces your hand. If you can't patch this week, audit which Nexus switches have NX-API or OAM enabled, and turn off what you're not using. Attack surface you don't need is attack surface you shouldn't be carrying.

Turning to Google. According to reporting from The Hacker News and BleepingComputer, citing Google's own disclosure, attackers hijacked the domain name system records for three country-code domains — Ghana's dot-g-h, Sierra Leone's dot-s-l, and American Samoa's dot-a-s — and used that control to obtain real, legitimately-issued certificates for Google domains. Certificate Transparency logs, the public ledger every browser checks to catch exactly this, show at least twelve fraudulent certificates issued between September 22nd and 27th. Google itself was never breached; the registries managing those three domains were. My take: this is supply-chain risk one layer below where most teams look. You can harden your own domain and your own certificate authority account perfectly and still get burned if a registry somewhere in the chain of trust gets compromised. The unglamorous defense is watching Certificate Transparency logs for every domain your organization owns, including the ones nobody's logged into in years.

Same thread both stories tonight: a feature or a domain you forgot was still turned on is exactly where an attacker looks first.
