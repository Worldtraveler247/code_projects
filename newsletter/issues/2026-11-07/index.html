Current Events

The Google Cloud Service Control Outage

When one of the biggest cloud providers in the world goes down, you'd assume it took a hacker to do it. On June 12th, 2025, it didn't. A single piece of blank, malformed configuration data knocked Google Cloud offline across the globe for hours -- no attacker, no breach, just one unvalidated change replicating everywhere at once. Here's how a quota check meant to protect the system became the thing that took it down.

Service Control is the regional component inside Google Cloud that checks quota and policy limits before a request even reaches the actual service behind it. According to Google's own incident report, on May 29th, 2025 -- about two weeks before the outage -- engineers added a new feature to Service Control for additional quota-policy checks. That new code path shipped without proper error handling and without being protected behind a feature flag, even though Google says the change did ship with an emergency "red button" that could disable it if something went wrong.

Here's the chain. On June 12th, around 10:45 in the morning Pacific time, a policy change was written into the regional database Service Control reads from -- and that policy data contained unintended blank fields. Because quota-policy data is treated as global, that blank entry replicated to every region within seconds. Service Control's new code path hit the blank data, threw a null-pointer error, and crashed. Every regional instance reading the same bad data crashed the same way, almost at once, which is why dozens of Google Cloud services -- and products that depend on them, from Cloudflare to Spotify to Discord -- went down together, worldwide.

Root cause, in one sentence: a single global configuration write hit an unvalidated, unflagged code path inside a control-plane service, and nothing in the deployment process stopped one bad write from taking down every region at once.

What actually held: Google's own site-reliability engineers were triaging within about two minutes of the first alert and had the root cause identified within roughly ten. The fix was that pre-built red button -- a kill switch disabling the faulty code path -- which Google says finished rolling out within about forty minutes, after which most regions began recovering. One region, Iowa, took longer, because its policy database was overloaded by the flood of retries during recovery.

So what would a senior engineer take from this one? A senior engineer treats a feature flag and a staged rollout as mandatory for any change to a control-plane or quota-enforcement service, not optional hygiene -- because this incident shows a two-week-old, unflagged code path was the entire blast radius for a global provider. On an AWS or Kubernetes lab, that's the exact habit worth practicing: gating a new policy or admission-control change behind a flag that can be flipped off in seconds, and testing that malformed input gets rejected outright instead of crashing the service that reads it.

What Held

  • Fast detection and triage. Google's SRE team was actively triaging within about two minutes of the first alert and had the root cause pinned down within roughly ten — fast enough that this stayed a multi-hour outage instead of a multi-day one.
  • A pre-built kill switch. Even though the new code path itself wasn't feature-flagged, Google had shipped it alongside a separate "red button" that could disable the faulty serving path entirely once engineers knew what to flip.
  • A transparent, detailed public report. Google published a root-cause incident report within days, naming the specific code path, the missing protections, and concrete remediation commitments — the kind of detail that lets the rest of the industry actually learn from the failure instead of guessing at it.

Senior Security Advice

Enforce DMARC with a Reject Policy to Stop Email Domain Spoofing

The Advice

Publish a DMARC record with a "reject" policy for every domain your organization owns, including domains that never send mail, so nobody can forge your name into a fraud email.

Why It Matters

Email headers are trivially easy to forge. Without DMARC enforcement, anyone on the internet can send a message that shows your company's own domain in the "from" field, and most mail servers will deliver it straight to an inbox. That gap is the engine behind business email compromise: an attacker spoofs the CFO's address and tells accounts payable to wire money, or spoofs the IT helpdesk to harvest employee passwords. These attacks don't need malware or a network foothold, only a convincing forged sender, which is exactly why they remain one of the most expensive fraud categories organizations face.

The incident class this guards against is business email compromise and vendor-impersonation fraud: a forged message that looks like it came from inside the house, used to move money or credentials before anyone double-checks. The US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency required DMARC enforcement across all federal executive branch domains under Binding Operational Directive 18-01 for exactly this reason, and NIST's guidance on trustworthy email lays out the same control as core defense against spoofed mail.

How to Implement It

  1. Inventory every domain the organization owns, including parked, retired, and marketing-only domains. Unmonitored domains are the easiest ones for an attacker to spoof, because nobody is watching them.
  2. Publish a DMARC record at policy "none" first, with aggregate reporting turned on, and review those reports to identify every legitimate system sending mail from that domain: payroll, ticketing, marketing platforms, transactional mail.
  3. Authenticate each legitimate sender with SPF and DKIM so it passes DMARC on its own merits, then move the policy to "quarantine" for a monitoring period before advancing to "reject."
  4. Set an explicit subdomain policy rather than relying on inheritance, and apply "reject" immediately to any domain that sends no mail at all — there is no legitimate traffic to protect, so there is no reason to wait.
  5. Verify the control is actually working: send a test message from an unauthorized source and confirm the receiving side bounces or rejects it, and keep pulling the aggregate reports afterward to confirm real mail isn't failing and that rejected volume reflects actual spoofing attempts rather than a missed sending source.

The Pushback You'll Hear

"This will break legitimate mail — our mailing lists and vendor tools will start failing." That risk is real if you skip straight to "reject," but it's a sequencing problem, not a reason to avoid the control. The phased rollout through monitoring and quarantine exists specifically to surface every legitimate sender before enforcement bites, and a percentage rollout tag lets you enforce on a slice of traffic while you finish the inventory. The actual risk is leaving the policy at "none" indefinitely, which reports on spoofing without stopping any of it.

Tech News

This week, a network-switch vendor shipped a fix for a flaw that needed zero credentials to take over the device — and separately, Red Hat patched a container bug that let a single command quietly cancel the safety rules you thought you'd locked in. Two stories today, and both are about the same blind spot: a control you assume is enforced isn't always enforced the way you think.

Start with Zyxel. According to Zyxel's own firmware release notes, corroborated by CVE tracking from Feedly and The Hacker Wire, the company shipped version 2.90 Patch 3 for its GS1900 series switches on November 3rd, fixing CVE-2026-107441 — a pre-authentication, out-of-bounds write in the device's CGI management interface. Pre-authentication means an attacker never has to log in at all. It carries a CVSS score of 8.8, it hits ten different GS1900 models, and Zyxel hasn't reported it as exploited in the wild yet — this is a patch-now-before-it-becomes-a-headline situation, not an active-breach one. Here's my take: a senior engineer treats "not yet exploited" on an unauthenticated, network-facing management flaw as a countdown, not a pass — these switches sit at the edge of a lot of small-business and branch networks, exactly where nobody's watching the logs closely.

Next up, turning to containers. According to Red Hat's own bug-tracking advisory, corroborated by independent vulnerability research from Endor Labs, Red Hat shipped a fix last month for CVE-2026-94603, a maximum-severity flaw rated a perfect 10.0 in Podman, the container engine. Here's the mechanism: if a container image carries a checkpoint-restore annotation, running it with the podman run command silently ignores restrictions the operator explicitly set — things like dropped Linux capabilities — and restores the container with its original, more permissive privileges instead. The fix landed in Podman 5.8.8 and 6.1.3. My read: this is a sandbox bypass hiding inside a legitimate feature — checkpoint and restore exists to save and resume containers — and it's a reminder that a security control configured at launch time isn't guaranteed to survive every code path the runtime supports.

So what's the through-line tonight? Whether it's a switch's web interface or a container runtime's restore path, the senior move is the same: ask what happens when the system takes a shortcut around the control you assumed was permanent, then go verify it actually held.

Patch your GS1900 firmware, and check your Podman version against 5.8.8 or 6.1.3 before you trust another checkpointed image.
