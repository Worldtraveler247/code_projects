Current Events

The Change Healthcare Ransomware Attack

In February 2024, one stolen password with no second login check took down prescription processing and medical billing for a huge share of the US healthcare system, for weeks. No exotic exploit, no zero-day -- just a remote-access login that only ever asked for a username and a password. Here's how it actually happened.

Change Healthcare processes something like half of all medical claims in the United States -- the invisible plumbing connecting pharmacies, hospitals, and insurance companies. According to sworn testimony from UnitedHealth Group's own CEO before Congress, an affiliate of the ALPHV ransomware group, also known as BlackCat, got in on February 12th, 2024, by logging into a Citrix remote-access portal with compromised credentials. That portal had no multi-factor authentication turned on, so a stolen username and password was the entire barrier between the open internet and the network.

Here's the chain. Once inside, the attacker had roughly nine days -- until February 21st -- to move laterally through Change Healthcare's systems undetected, reaching far enough to exfiltrate what would later be confirmed as the largest healthcare data breach in US history. Then they deployed ransomware, and Change Healthcare pulled its own systems offline to contain the damage -- which is exactly what stopped prescription processing, insurance eligibility checks, and claims payment across the country for weeks.

Here's the root cause in one sentence: a single remote-access credential with no multi-factor authentication behind it was, by itself, enough to reach a network that handled sensitive health data for most of the country.

What made recovery so slow is its own separate lesson. The CEO told Congress the ransomware also locked up backup systems that Change Healthcare had built years before UnitedHealth acquired the company in 2022 -- backup infrastructure that had never actually been folded into UnitedHealth's own security standards after the deal closed. The system meant to make recovery fast was itself sitting inside the same blast radius as everything else.

Here's what held, and it's worth naming plainly: once the intrusion was confirmed, UnitedHealth Group did something this industry doesn't always manage -- public congressional testimony under oath, a detailed technical accounting to federal regulators, and a breach count that kept getting corrected upward in public as the true scope became clear, eventually landing near 190 million people. A company that lowballs or stonewalls its own breach number makes every downstream fix harder to trust; this one didn't do that.

Here's what a senior engineer, or a senior ISSO, would change in how they work because of this. Multi-factor authentication on any remote-access gateway isn't a nice-to-have configured "eventually" -- it belongs there from day one, enforced by policy, not left to hope. And after any acquisition, a senior engineer treats the acquired company's infrastructure, especially anything backup- or recovery-related, as unverified until it's been audited against the parent organization's own security baseline -- because a backup system that was never actually checked is not a backup system you can trust to be there when you need it.

What Held

  • Eventual transparency, even when it was costly. UnitedHealth Group's CEO testified under oath before Congress and walked through the technical root cause in public, rather than issuing only a vague statement — a meaningfully higher bar than many breached organizations clear.
  • A federal regulatory backstop existed. HHS's Office for Civil Rights required a final, corrected breach count, which is what eventually produced the accurate ~192.7 million figure instead of letting an early, understated number stand as the public record.
  • The company had the resources to fund emergency provider relief. UnitedHealth Group advanced billions of dollars to affected pharmacies and providers to keep prescriptions and payments moving during the outage — a mitigation available to few organizations this size, but real, and worth naming as the thing that kept the disruption from becoming a full-blown care crisis on top of a security incident.

Senior Security Advice

Break-Glass Emergency Access Accounts

The Advice

Stand up at least two dedicated break-glass emergency access accounts with permanent admin rights and hardware-key authentication, then treat every single sign-in attempt on them as an active incident.

Why It Matters

Modern admin access is layered on purpose: single sign-on, multi-factor authentication (MFA), and conditional access policies that check device health and location before letting anyone in. That layering is also a single point of failure. An identity provider outage, a botched conditional access change, or a mass credential reset can lock every real administrator out of their own tenant at the exact moment someone needs to get in and fix it. That is the incident class break-glass accounts exist to prevent: total lockout during a crisis, with no way to respond because the fix itself requires the access that just disappeared. The quieter failure mode is the opposite problem — an old "just in case" admin account that nobody rotated, nobody monitored, and nobody remembered exists, sitting in the directory as an unwatched door an attacker can walk through with none of the alerting that would catch a normal admin login.

How to Implement It

  1. Create at least two cloud-only, non-personal emergency access accounts, hosted on the default tenant domain rather than synced from on-premises directory, each permanently assigned the highest admin role directly — not activated through a privileged identity management workflow, since that activation step is exactly what an outage would break.
  2. Register a phishing-resistant, hardware-backed credential (a FIDO2 security key) for each account, distinct from the authentication method everyday admins use, and store the physical keys separately with split custody so no single person controls both.
  3. Exclude these accounts from any conditional access policy that could ever block their sign-in — an enforced policy protecting against the exact scenario the account exists for defeats the purpose.
  4. Configure alerting so that every sign-in and audit log event from a break-glass account — successful or failed — fires an immediate, high-priority alert to the security team, since legitimate use should be rare enough that any use at all is worth a page.
  5. Verify the control on a fixed schedule, not just after setup: actually sign in with the account in a controlled window, confirm the alert fires, confirm access still works end-to-end, and rotate the credential immediately after every real or test use.

The Pushback You'll Hear

"We already have single sign-on and conditional access — isn't a bypass account just a backdoor?" The senior-engineer rebuttal: the difference between a backdoor and a break-glass account is visibility. A backdoor is undocumented and unwatched. A break-glass account is documented, defended by a hardware key nobody else uses, physically split between custodians, and wired to alert the moment it's touched. The real backdoor risk is what teams build instead when they skip this step — an admin's personal account with saved credentials and no monitoring, kept "just in case."

Senior Talk Tracks

Idempotency

Here's a word that sounds like it belongs in a math class, but it's actually one of the most important ideas in automation: idempotency. If you've ever run the same script twice and gotten two different, messy results, you've felt the absence of it firsthand.

Plain English first. Idempotent means running an operation once and running it fifty times produces the exact same end state. Not "it runs fifty times without crashing" -- the actual state of the system after run one and after run fifty is identical. "Make sure this file exists with this content" is idempotent. "Append this line to this file" is not -- run that one fifty times and you've got fifty duplicate lines.

Here's why it matters in production. Automation tools like Ansible are built entirely around this idea: a playbook is supposed to describe the end state you want, not a list of one-time actions to perform. That means you can run the exact same playbook against a server that's brand new, a server that's half-configured, or a server you already ran it against yesterday, and get the same safe result every time -- no manual bookkeeping about what's already been done.

The senior take, the part a junior usually misses: idempotency isn't really about convenience, it's about trust. If your automation is idempotent, you can re-run it after any failure, any network blip, any half-finished deploy, without worrying you'll make things worse. If it's not idempotent, every re-run is a gamble, so people get scared of re-running it -- and a tool nobody trusts to re-run quietly turns back into manual work during every incident, which is exactly when you need automation the most.

Think of a thermostat instead of a light switch. Flipping a light switch is an action -- do it twice and the light is now back off. A thermostat set to seventy degrees is idempotent -- you can walk over and set it to seventy a hundred times, and the room just stays at seventy. Good automation is a thermostat, not a light switch.

A natural way to raise this with a colleague is: "if that script failed halfway through, are we confident we can just run it again, or does someone need to go clean something up first?"

Key Takeaways

  1. Idempotent means running an operation once or fifty times leaves the system in the exact same end state, not just "doesn't crash on repeat."
  2. Ansible playbooks are built around describing a desired end state specifically so they can be re-run safely after any failure.
  3. Automation nobody trusts to re-run safely quietly turns back into manual work during the exact incidents where automation matters most.

Reader Question

"If idempotency is so important, why do so many scripts people write still not have it?"

Honestly, it's because non-idempotent scripts are faster to write and often work fine the first time, so the gap only shows up later, during a re-run nobody planned for — usually mid-incident, which is the worst possible time to discover it. The fix isn't demanding perfect idempotency everywhere on day one; it's asking the idempotency question specifically for anything that's going to run more than once or get automated into a pipeline, since that's exactly where a non-idempotent script eventually bites someone.

Tech News

One HTTP request, no login required — that's all it took this week to read any file off a GitLab server, including the credentials your build pipeline runs on. If you administer software delivery pipelines, or you're aiming to, today's for you. Two stories: that GitLab flaw, and a new European law that just started a legal clock on how fast the next one has to be reported.

Start with GitLab. Researchers found a path-traversal bug in GitLab's commits API — the part of GitLab that serves up repository file information — tracked as CVE-2026-85706, and it scores a perfect ten out of ten on the Common Vulnerability Scoring System. An unauthenticated attacker sends one crafted request and reads any file the GitLab service account can see: source code, SSH keys, access tokens, the CI/CD secrets your build pipeline uses to deploy everything downstream. GitLab shipped a fix on September 10th. According to the security firm watchTowr, attackers were already probing for the flaw against its honeypot network within hours of that patch going out, and CISA added it to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog the next day, flagging it for mandatory forensic triage under a binding directive — meaning federal agencies aren't just told to patch, they're told to go check whether they were already compromised first. Here's my read: a patch tells you the hole is closed, not that nobody walked through it while it was open. If you run GitLab and you're only now applying the fix, don't stop there — check your access logs for that commits API endpoint before assuming you're clean.

Next up, a story about the clock itself. As of September 11th, the European Union's Cyber Resilience Act requires any manufacturer selling a product with digital elements into the EU — hardware or software — to report an actively exploited vulnerability within 24 hours of discovering it, a fuller technical notification within 72 hours, and a final report within 14 days. And this isn't a future-products rule — it covers what's already on the market today. My take: this changes the math for a lot of companies that treated "we'll patch it eventually and mention it in a blog post" as good enough. A 24-hour reporting clock means you need to already know how you'd detect exploitation and who internally gets the call before the vulnerability shows up, because you can't report what you can't first detect.

Through-line: whether it's a patch window or a legal deadline, today's lesson is the same — the clock that matters most starts the moment something goes wrong, not when you get around to noticing.
