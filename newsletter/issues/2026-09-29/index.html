Current Events

The LastPass Vault Breach

If you've ever been told "don't worry, it's encrypted," here's the incident that puts that comfort to the test. A password manager used by tens of millions of people got breached twice in four months — and the second breach only happened because of the first. Here's how it actually happened.

On August 8th, 2022, an attacker compromised a LastPass software engineer's corporate laptop and used it to reach a cloud-based development environment. Over four days, before the intrusion was cut off, the attacker made off with source code and internal technical documentation — fourteen of the company's roughly two hundred code repositories. LastPass said no customer data was touched in this first breach, and closed out its investigation with help from Mandiant about five weeks later. Case closed, or so it seemed.

It wasn't closed — it was reconnaissance. The attacker used that stolen source code and internal documentation to go after a specific target: a LastPass DevOps engineer, one of only a handful of people with the credentials to decrypt the company's cloud storage backups. The attacker exploited a vulnerability in third-party media-software running on that engineer's home computer to plant a keylogger, then waited. When the engineer authenticated to LastPass's cloud environment, the attacker captured his master password after it had already cleared multi-factor authentication, and used it to access and decrypt cloud storage volumes containing backups of customer vault data.

Root cause, in one sentence: a single engineer's personal computer, running unpatched third-party software, held the keys that unlocked backups of millions of customers' password vaults — with no separation between his personal environment and that level of corporate access.

In December, LastPass disclosed that the attacker had exfiltrated encrypted backups of customer vaults — usernames, passwords, secure notes, form-fill data — along with some things that weren't encrypted at all, including the website URLs saved inside each vault. What actually held here was LastPass's own encryption design: vaults are locked with a key derived from each user's master password through a slow, repeated hashing process, and LastPass itself never has access to that master password. For customers with a strong master password and a modern iteration count, that encryption bought real time and real protection. But — and this needs saying plainly — LastPass had let iteration counts drift downward for some older accounts for years, and a weak master password on a weakly-iterated vault was a genuinely crackable target, not just a theoretical one.

Here's what a senior engineer, or a senior ISSO, takes from this. The failure wasn't the encryption — the encryption is what limited the damage. The failure was that one home computer, outside any corporate patch or monitoring program, held the actual keys to production backups. In a RHEL 9 lab with Podman, that means treating any credential capable of decrypting backups as a Tier Zero asset: it lives on hardened, monitored infrastructure only, never on a personal device running whatever consumer software happens to be installed.

What Held

  • Zero-knowledge, client-side encryption. LastPass never held customer master passwords, and vault encryption keys were derived locally from each user's master password through repeated hashing. For customers with a strong master password and a current iteration count, that design meant the attacker walked away with encrypted blobs, not usable credentials.
  • Naming the limits of that control is the senior read. LastPass had allowed iteration counts on some older accounts to remain at long-outdated, weaker defaults, and a weak master password on one of those accounts was realistically crackable. The control held unevenly, based on choices made years earlier — which is exactly the kind of nuance worth saying out loud instead of flattening into "it was encrypted, so it was fine."

Senior Security Advice

Kill Mandatory Password Rotation, Screen Against Breach Lists Instead

The Advice

Stop forcing scheduled password changes, and instead require every new password to be checked against a list of known-breached and commonly-used passwords the moment it's set.

Why It Matters

For two decades, "change your password every 90 days" was standard policy. It backfires. When people are forced to rotate a password on a timer, they don't pick something new and strong — they make the smallest change that satisfies the policy, turning "Summer2024!" into "Summer2025!" next quarter. That pattern is predictable enough that an attacker who recovers an old password can often guess the current one. Meanwhile, forced rotation does nothing about the actual way most account takeovers happen today: credential stuffing, where attackers take usernames and passwords leaked from one breach and replay them against other sites, betting on password reuse. That's the incident class that keeps showing up in breach reports year after year — not "the password was too old," but "the password was already public." NIST's Digital Identity Guidelines caught up to this reality: SP 800-63B (Revision 4) now states that verifiers and credential service providers SHALL NOT require subscribers to change passwords periodically, and instead SHALL force a change only when there's evidence the credential has actually been compromised. In its place, NIST requires screening new passwords against lists of known-breached, commonly-used, and predictable values at the moment they're chosen.

How to Implement It

  1. Remove mandatory periodic expiration from the password policy and identity provider (Active Directory fine-grained password policy, Okta, Entra ID) — passwords change only on evidence of compromise, not a calendar.
  2. Add breach-password screening at password-set time, using something like the Have I Been Pwned Pwned Passwords API (k-anonymity model, safe to query without exposing the full password) or a built-in compromised-credential check in your identity provider.
  3. Replace complexity gymnastics (mandatory special characters, forced digit swaps) with a minimum-length requirement — NIST recommends 15+ characters for privileged accounts — paired with MFA, since length plus screening beats "P@ssw0rd1!" every time.
  4. Verify the control is actually working, not just configured: in a staging environment, attempt to set a password that's a known public breach value and confirm it's rejected; separately confirm the identity provider logs show zero forced changes triggered by a calendar and only changes triggered by compromise signals.
  5. Track helpdesk password-reset ticket volume before and after rollout — a real, working control should show that number drop, since users are no longer locked out by expired passwords they forgot after a forced change.

The Pushback You'll Hear

"Our compliance framework still requires 90-day rotation, we can't just turn it off." The senior-engineer rebuttal: check what the framework actually cites — most authentication requirements in modern compliance mappings defer to NIST SP 800-63B, and that document has moved from discouraging rotation to explicitly prohibiting it. If an auditor is working from an outdated checklist item, bring the current standard into the conversation and document the exception with the breach-screening control as the compensating measure. A password that's screened against every known breach list in real time is a stronger control than one that just happens to be 89 days old.

Senior Talk Tracks

Why Agent Tool-Use Expands the Attack Surface

An AI chatbot that can only answer questions is basically harmless — worst case, it says something wrong. An AI agent is a different animal entirely, because an agent doesn't just answer, it acts: it can browse the web, read your files, send emails, run code, or call other tools on your behalf. The moment you give a language model the ability to actually do things, you've also given it a whole new set of doors an attacker can walk through.

Here's the plain version. Every tool you connect to an AI agent — a web browser, a code interpreter, a database, an email client — is a new capability, and every capability is also a new attack surface. If an agent can browse the web to help you, then a malicious webpage can potentially plant hidden instructions in its own text, instructions the agent might follow without ever checking with you first. That's not a hypothetical edge case anymore; it's an active, named category of attack.

Here's why it matters in production, and the senior take: a junior engineer evaluates an AI agent by asking "does it complete the task correctly?" A senior engineer asks a second question nobody put on the product demo: "what's the worst thing this agent could be tricked into doing with the tools I just gave it?" More tools genuinely means more capability, but it also means a wider blast radius the moment something goes wrong — and unlike a human employee, an agent can't always tell the difference between an instruction from you and an instruction hidden inside a document it just read.

Think of it like handing a new employee a master key on their first day because it's convenient, instead of giving them access to only the rooms their job actually requires. It saves you a conversation today. It's also exactly how one compromised room becomes access to the whole building.

A natural way to open this at work: "Before we wire this agent up to another tool, what's it allowed to do without asking us first — and have we actually tested what happens if it gets fed a prompt it wasn't supposed to trust?"

Key Takeaways

  1. Every tool connected to an AI agent is both new capability and new attack surface, because the agent can't always distinguish your instructions from ones hidden in content it processes.
  2. The senior question isn't "can the agent complete the task," it's "what's the worst thing it could be tricked into doing with the tools it has."
  3. Scoping an agent's tool access to the minimum a task requires limits blast radius the same way least privilege does for human accounts.

Reader Question

"If tool-use is what makes an AI agent actually useful, isn't limiting its tools just making it worse at its job?"

I'd say there's a real tradeoff, but it's not an all-or-nothing choice — you can scope an agent's tools tightly to exactly what a given task needs, the same way you'd scope a human employee's access, rather than either giving it everything or nothing. My answer would be that an agent with five well-chosen tools and clear boundaries on what it can do without confirmation is usually more useful in practice than one with twenty tools and no guardrails, because the second one gets pulled from production the first time it does something nobody wanted.

Tech News

Two enterprise products you've probably never heard of by name just landed on the federal government's must-patch-now list — and one of them quietly powers online stores you've shopped at this month. Two stories tonight: an update on yesterday's NetScaler zero-day, and a fresh pair of vulnerabilities hitting an API gateway and an e-commerce platform at the same time.

First, the update. Yesterday I told you about two unpatched Citrix NetScaler flaws under active attack with no CVE number yet. Here's what changed: those flaws now have names — CVE-2026-88771 and CVE-2026-88772 — Citrix has shipped a fix, and CISA added both to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on September 27th, ordering every federal civilian agency to patch on an emergency timeline. Here's my read: a KEV listing doesn't mean the danger just started — it means the government is now treating it as a known, ongoing fact of life. If you manage a NetScaler box, patching is step one. Hunting your logs for signs someone already got in before the patch existed is step two, and skipping it is the mistake that turns a vulnerability into a breach.

Next up, a story from the same playbook, different vendors. CISA also added CVE-2026-5430, a path-traversal flaw in WSO2's API Control Plane and API Manager, and CVE-2026-71362, a broken-authorization bug in Adobe Commerce and Magento, to that same exploited-vulnerabilities catalog. The WSO2 flaw lets an attacker upload a file outside where it belongs and run code on the server. The Adobe Commerce flaw lets an attacker reach data they were never supposed to see, without even logging in. Researchers say attackers were quietly probing for the WSO2 bug more than a week before CISA's warning went out. My take: notice the pattern across both of tonight's stories — in every case, exploitation started before the public advisory did. A senior engineer doesn't wait for the CVE number to start watching; the CVE number is just when the rest of the world catches up to what attackers already knew.

Both stories tonight land on the same lesson: patch management isn't a monthly chore, it's a race you're usually already running late in.
