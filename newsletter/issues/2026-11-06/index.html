Current Events

Oracle E-Business Suite Zero-Day

A piece of enterprise software most employees never think about -- the system that runs invoicing, payroll, and supply chain for thousands of companies -- turned out to have a hole big enough to walk through without logging in at all. Today: the Oracle E-Business Suite zero-day, and how an extortion crew turned it into a mass data-theft campaign before most victims even knew they'd been touched.

Oracle E-Business Suite, EBS for short, is a set of enterprise resource-planning applications that many large and mid-size organizations run to handle finance, HR, and operations. According to Oracle's own security alert advisory, a critical flaw tracked as CVE-2025-61882 sat in EBS's Concurrent Processing component -- specifically its BI Publisher Integration -- and it carried a CVSS score of 9.8 out of 10: remotely exploitable, over the network, with no authentication required at all.

Here's the chain. Security researchers at watchTowr have described this as five distinct bugs, chained together, to reach full, pre-authentication remote code execution on the server -- not one simple flaw, but a sequence of smaller weaknesses that only becomes dangerous when used in the right order. According to Google's Mandiant threat intelligence team, as reported by multiple outlets, attackers linked to the Cl0p extortion group had already been exploiting this chain as a zero-day since on or around August 9th of 2025 -- weeks before Oracle even knew about it. By late September, victim organizations started receiving extortion emails claiming their EBS data had been stolen. Oracle confirmed the attempted extortion on October 3rd and rushed out an emergency, out-of-band patch the very next day. CISA added the flaw to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog just two days after that, giving federal agencies until October 27th to patch.

Root cause, in one sentence: an internet-facing enterprise application exposed a chain of unauthenticated code paths that nobody had to log in to reach, and it sat exploitable for roughly two months before any public patch existed.

What actually held: once Oracle and Mandiant understood the chain, the response moved fast -- an emergency patch within about 24 hours of Oracle's own confirmation of exploitation, and a same-week listing in CISA's catalog that forced federal remediation on a hard deadline instead of a routine patch cycle. Oracle also says part of this attack chain involved vulnerabilities already addressed in its July 2025 Critical Patch Update, so organizations that were current on that routine update were already partway protected before the emergency patch even existed.

So what would a senior engineer flag from this one? A senior engineer treats an internet-facing ERP system as a priority patch target precisely because of what it holds -- financial and HR data -- and never assumes a vendor's last routine patch cycle means an application is fully current; the standing practice is to verify installed versions against the vendor's latest advisory directly, not trust a patch ticket's status field. On a RHEL or AWS lab, that means practicing exactly that: building a quick version-check step that confirms what's actually installed against what the vendor currently says is patched, instead of assuming "patched last quarter" still means "patched today."

What Held

  • A fast emergency patch once confirmed. Oracle shipped an out-of-band advisory and patch within roughly a day of publicly confirming the extortion reports, rather than waiting for its next scheduled quarterly cycle.
  • Rapid, forcing-function disclosure. CISA's KEV addition came within two days of Oracle's patch and carried a hard federal remediation deadline, which pushes organizations to act on a fixed timeline instead of an open-ended "when convenient."
  • Partial protection from routine patch hygiene. Oracle's own statement that part of the chain was addressed in the July 2025 CPU means organizations that keep current on routine vendor patch cycles were not starting from zero exposure.

Senior Security Advice

Alert on Impossible Travel and Anomalous Sign-In Behavior

The Advice

Turn on impossible-travel and atypical sign-in risk detection for every identity provider you run, and route every one of those alerts to a human who has to look at it within the hour.

Why It Matters

A stolen password or hijacked session token doesn't look like an attack to most systems — it looks like a normal login, because it is one, just from the wrong person. If nobody is watching where and when sign-ins happen, an attacker with valid stolen credentials can sit inside an account indefinitely with nothing else going wrong.

The incident class this guards against is business email compromise and account takeover: an attacker signs in to a mailbox or cloud account from a location the real user has never used, then sets up mailbox forwarding rules, watches for financial conversations, or pivots to other systems. These intrusions routinely run for weeks before anyone notices, because the sign-in itself never triggered anything. Impossible-travel detection — two sign-ins from locations too far apart to reach in the elapsed time — and atypical-travel detection — a location unusual for that specific user — exist to surface that gap. Microsoft's Entra ID Identity Protection documentation describes both as standing risk detections built for this exact pattern, with atypical travel calculated to filter out obvious false positives like VPN exits already tied to other people in the organization.

How to Implement It

  1. Turn on risk-based sign-in detection in your identity provider — impossible travel and atypical travel at minimum — and forward every risk event to your SIEM, not just the provider's own dashboard.
  2. Define named or trusted network locations for offices, VPN egress ranges, and known remote-work regions so routine travel doesn't flood the queue with noise.
  3. Make the response automatic, not advisory: a flagged high-risk sign-in should trigger a step-up authentication challenge or a session block, not just a log entry someone might read later.
  4. Build a real on-call path for these alerts, with a defined triage window, so a 2 a.m. impossible-travel hit doesn't wait until Monday.
  5. Verify it's actually working: sign in to a test account through a VPN exit node in a different country and confirm three things — the event is flagged as risky, the step-up challenge actually fires, and the alert lands in the on-call channel rather than an inbox nobody checks.

The Pushback You'll Hear

"This just generates a wall of false positives from people on VPNs and business travel." That's real, but it's a tuning problem, not a reason to skip the control. Named locations and risk-based conditional access — challenge instead of hard-block on medium risk — bring the noise down without turning the signal off. The alternative isn't a quieter queue; it's flying blind on one of the clearest signals available for catching account takeover before an attacker gets to the part where they move money or data.

Tech News

A maximum-severity flaw in GitLab — the platform tens of thousands of engineering teams use to build and ship their code — is being actively exploited right now, no login required. And separately, a Russian state-linked hacking group found a much quieter way in: they didn't attack a target directly, they poisoned a piece of analytics code that target's own website was already trusting. Two stories tonight, and both are really the same lesson wearing different clothes — the thing you trust by default is exactly where an attacker wants to live.

Start with GitLab. According to GitLab's own security advisory, corroborated by BleepingComputer and CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, the flaw is CVE-2026-97229, an integer overflow in the regular-expression engine GitLab's CI/CD pipelines use to validate configuration files — CI/CD, short for continuous integration and continuous delivery, the automated pipeline that builds and deploys code. GitLab rates it a perfect 10.0, and the ugly part is that it doesn't require a login: a specially crafted regular expression in a pipeline file is enough to run code on the server. GitLab shipped fixes in mid-October, CISA has confirmed active exploitation, and Shadowserver was still tracking over a thousand exposed instances as of late October. Here's my take: a senior engineer doesn't just ask "is this patched" — they ask what that server could reach if it's popped, because a compromised CI/CD server doesn't just lose data, it can inject malicious code into everything it builds afterward.

Next up, turning to the Apple story. According to reporting from The Hacker News, corroborated by multiple security outlets covering the French firm Sekoia's research, a campaign researchers are calling "Poisoned Source" compromised a third-party analytics script — one that was running on websites those sites never thought to double-check. Visitors on Android and iOS got redirected into exploit chains, including a years-old, already-patched Apple WebKit flaw, CVE-2024-27834, which CISA has now added to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog with a November 18th federal deadline. Sekoia links the activity to APT29 with moderate-to-high confidence, though CISA and Apple haven't made that attribution themselves. My read: this is a watering-hole attack in the truest sense — the hackers never touched the target's network, they just waited at a well everyone already drinks from.

So what does that mean for you? A senior engineer treats every embedded third-party script and every CI/CD pipeline as part of the attack surface, not outside it — that's the through-line tonight.
