Current Events

SolarWinds SUNBURST Supply Chain Attack

Picture a software update -- the thing you install specifically because you trust the company that signed it -- and now picture that exact update quietly carrying a backdoor straight past every perimeter defense you own, because it arrived through the one channel you never thought to question. That's SUNBURST, and in December 2020 it turned out to be sitting inside network-monitoring software used by thousands of organizations, including multiple US federal agencies. Here's how it actually happened.

SolarWinds Orion is network-monitoring software, the kind of tool that needs broad, trusted access across a network just to do its job -- watching servers, routers, and applications for problems. According to CISA's own emergency directive, attackers compromised SolarWinds' software build environment itself and inserted malicious code, later named SUNBURST, directly into legitimate Orion software updates using a tool SolarWinds calls SUNSPOT. Customers who installed what they believed was a routine, digitally-signed update were actually installing a backdoor, signed with SolarWinds' own valid certificate.

Root cause, in one sentence: a trusted software vendor's own build pipeline was compromised, which means every customer downstream inherited that compromise automatically, through a channel -- a signed vendor update -- that security tools are specifically designed to trust rather than inspect.

Here's what the backdoor actually let attackers do once installed: SUNBURST stayed dormant for up to two weeks, then quietly reached out to a command-and-control domain disguised to look like ordinary Orion traffic. From there, according to CISA's updated guidance, the actor moved laterally using other techniques entirely -- including forging SAML authentication tokens to impersonate legitimate users -- in incidents where SolarWinds wasn't even the way in. CISA's emergency directive, issued the same day the compromise became public, ordered every federal civilian agency to disconnect or power down any Orion installation immediately and treat every host that software had monitored as potentially compromised already.

What contained it, and this is the senior detail worth naming: FireEye, investigating its own breach, is the organization that found this -- not SolarWinds, and not any single customer's own security team. FireEye traced its own stolen red-team tools back to a wider compromise and published that link publicly, which is what triggered CISA's directive the same day. A private incident-response team's own forensic discipline is what surfaced a nation-state supply-chain compromise that had likely been running since before early 2020.

So what does that mean for you? A senior engineer treats a vendor's signed update as trusted, not untouchable -- because this incident proves that "it's signed by the vendor" and "it's safe" are two different claims. On a RHEL and Podman lab, that means never letting a single automatic-update pipeline have blanket, unmonitored access across every host at once, and treating outbound traffic from a monitoring agent itself as something worth watching, not just the traffic it reports on.

Control Failures

2 Customers' security tooling and network monitoring implicitly trusted digitally-signed vendor updates with no additional behavioral inspection of what a "trusted" update actually did once installed. SI-7 — Software, Firmware, and Information Integrity
3 The compromised Orion software required broad network access and elevated privileges to function, so a single compromised component inherited reach across the entire monitored environment. AC-6 — Least Privilege

What Held

  • An external party's own forensic rigor surfaced the compromise. FireEye found this not by scanning for SolarWinds-specific indicators but by thoroughly investigating its own, unrelated breach and refusing to let the trail go cold — a reminder that incident response on your own systems can surface threats far bigger than your own incident.
  • A same-day emergency directive limited federal exposure. CISA's Emergency Directive 21-01 went out the same day the compromise went public, giving federal agencies an immediate, binding instruction rather than optional guidance to consider.
  • Say the limit of that out loud. The directive and the disclosure came only after the backdoor had already been live in production environments for months — "contained quickly once discovered" is not the same claim as "caught early," and naming that gap, rather than letting same-day response read as early detection, is the senior move.

Senior Security Advice

Deploy DNSSEC to Stop DNS Spoofing and Cache Poisoning

The Advice

Sign every domain you control with DNSSEC, and turn on DNSSEC validation on every recursive resolver your organization uses, so forged DNS answers get rejected instead of trusted.

Why It Matters

Plain DNS has no built-in way to prove an answer is genuine. A resolver asks "where does this name point?" and trusts whatever response arrives first, which is exactly what makes cache poisoning and DNS spoofing possible: an attacker who can inject a forged answer can silently redirect a login page, an API endpoint, or an email server to infrastructure they control, without ever touching the real target's systems. NIST's own DNS security guidance, SP 800-81 Revision 3, frames the core goal of securing DNS as protecting the integrity and authenticity of DNS information — in plain terms, making sure the answer you get is the answer the domain owner actually published. DNSSEC is the mechanism that delivers that: it cryptographically signs DNS records so a validating resolver can detect tampering and throw the forged answer away instead of handing it to a browser or application. This isn't a theoretical risk. Sustained DNS hijacking and tampering campaigns against government and private-sector domains were serious enough that the Department of Homeland Security issued an emergency directive specifically to get federal agencies auditing and locking down their DNS records. Without cryptographic validation, that kind of record tampering looks identical to a legitimate DNS answer, so nothing downstream catches it.

How to Implement It

  1. Enable DNSSEC signing on every authoritative zone you own. In AWS, Route 53's own developer guide walks through this directly: Route 53 manages the Zone Signing Key automatically, you manage the Key Signing Key in AWS KMS, and the two work together to sign the zone.
  2. Establish the chain of trust. Signing the zone isn't enough on its own — AWS's documentation is explicit that you must take the resulting Delegation Signer (DS) record to your domain registrar and publish it there, or validating resolvers have nothing to check your signature against.
  3. Turn on DNSSEC validation on your own recursive resolvers, not just signing on your outbound zones, so your own users are protected against forged answers from anyone else's compromised DNS, too.
  4. Automate and monitor key rotation. AWS's own guidance warns that failing to update the registrar with new key information during a rotation can break the chain of trust and make the domain unreachable for anyone using a validating resolver — this is the single most common way DNSSEC deployments go wrong, and it's a monitoring and automation problem, not a reason to skip signing.
  5. Verify it's actually working, not just enabled. Check signing status directly — in Route 53 that's the aws route53 get-dnssec command — and separately confirm resolution end to end with an independent tool like dig +dnssec checking for the AD (Authenticated Data) flag, or a public DNSSEC debugger. A signed zone with a broken chain of trust can quietly make your domain unreachable while looking "enabled" in your own console.

The Pushback You'll Hear

"DNSSEC is fragile — it's famous for breaking domains when key rotation goes wrong." That history is accurate, but the cause was always operational: someone rotated a key without updating the registrar, and the chain of trust snapped. The fix isn't avoiding DNSSEC, it's treating key rotation and DS-record sync as a monitored, automated pipeline the same way you'd treat TLS certificate renewal. Major providers now automate the hard parts specifically because this used to be a manual failure point.

Tech News

If a vendor's patch already shipped and you installed it, you'd assume you're covered. Two days ago I told you about a third, unpatched Ivanti Connect Secure flaw. Tonight: what happened when attackers chained it with two others — on devices that were already fully patched. Two stories: that Ivanti update, and a Patch Tuesday so big it breaks the way most teams think about patching at all.

Start with Ivanti. According to BleepingComputer's reporting on Volexity's research, a threat actor the researchers track as UTA0576 chained that third flaw — CVE-2026-104935 — together with two other Connect Secure vulnerabilities to get root-level code execution, starting around October 3rd. Here's the part that should get your attention: this wasn't happening on outdated boxes. Some of the devices Volexity examined were fully patched. The attacker also never dropped a web shell or malware onto the appliance — it simply re-exploited the device fresh for every single command it wanted to run, and it altered the device's own logs each time, specifically to blind Ivanti's built-in Integrity Checker Tool. CISA added this to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog with an October 16th deadline for federal agencies. My read: "fully patched" only covers the flaws a patch actually closes — it says nothing about flaws still chained in behind it. And a vendor's own integrity-checking tool is a signal you should pair with independent, off-box logging, not a guarantee you can lean on alone, because this attacker's whole method was designed specifically to blind that one tool.

Next up, Microsoft. According to BleepingComputer, this month's Patch Tuesday, released October 13th, fixed a record 1,411 flaws — one actively exploited zero-day, two more publicly disclosed, and 70 rated critical. My take: at that volume, "patch everything" isn't a strategy, it's a wish. The actual skill is triage — start from what CISA's KEV catalog says is already being exploited, layer in what's reachable from the internet, and work outward by how far a flaw could spread if it's hit, not by severity score alone.

Same thread tonight: whether it's one vulnerability chained three layers deep, or fourteen hundred flaws landing in a single release, the senior move is knowing what's actually exploitable before you decide what's urgent.
