Current Events

The F5 BIG-IP Source Code Theft

A nation-state hacking group sat inside one of the internet's most widely deployed network appliance vendors for the better part of a year — and walked out with the company's own source code and a list of bugs nobody outside F5 knew existed yet. This is the F5 BIG-IP breach, and it's a supply-chain story about what happens when the people who make your security hardware get hacked first.

F5 makes BIG-IP, a family of load-balancer and application-delivery appliances that sit at the edge of networks for banks, telecoms, and federal agencies — the kind of box that terminates traffic before it ever reaches an internal server. According to F5's own SEC filing, the company detected unauthorized, long-term access to its systems on August 9, 2025. The intruder — which F5 described only as a highly sophisticated nation-state actor, though outside reporting from Mandiant and Bloomberg ties the activity to a China-nexus group tracked as UNC5221 using a backdoor called BRICKSTORM — had been sitting inside F5's BIG-IP product development environment and its engineering knowledge-management platform. Reporting differs on exactly how long: Mandiant's own research says BRICKSTORM intrusions average roughly 393 days of undetected dwell time, and multiple outlets put this specific intrusion at around 12 months — but that duration comes from secondary reporting, not from F5's own filing, so treat it as reported, not confirmed by the company.

Here's the failure chain. The attacker got into F5's broader development environment and from there reached the systems where BIG-IP's actual source code lives, plus files describing vulnerabilities F5 had found internally but not yet patched or disclosed. They exfiltrated some of that source code, some undisclosed-vulnerability information, and — from the knowledge-management platform — configuration and implementation details for a small percentage of F5's customers.

Root cause, in one sentence: a nation-state actor got deep enough into F5's internal development network to reach source code and bug data, and stayed long enough that the company can't say with precision when access actually began.

What contained this, and this is the part worth naming in an interview: F5 stated it found no evidence the attacker modified its software supply chain — the source code itself, or the build and release pipeline that turns that code into the binaries customers actually install. The company also said the intruder never reached its NGINX product line, nor its CRM, financial, support-ticketing, or iHealth diagnostic systems. That's internal segmentation holding even after the perimeter around development had already failed. The U.S. Department of Justice authorized a disclosure delay under SEC rules so F5 could investigate and remediate before going public, and CISA issued Emergency Directive 26-01 the same day disclosure happened, giving federal agencies a concrete inventory-and-patch deadline instead of a vague warning.

The takeaway for a senior engineer: a vendor can be breached upstream of you, and the first thing worth checking isn't "did they get hacked" — it's "did they prove their build pipeline wasn't tampered with." A senior engineer auditing any vendor relationship after a disclosure like this pulls package and image checksums against the vendor's published hashes before trusting an update, the same discipline worth running cold, right now, against a RHEL or container image registry already in use.

What Held

F5 stated it found no evidence the attacker modified its software supply chain — the source code repositories or the build-and-release pipeline that compiles and signs customer-facing software — which is the control that let the company tell customers the updates they install are still trustworthy. The breach also did not reach F5's NGINX product line or its CRM, financial, support-case-management, and iHealth diagnostic systems, showing that internal segmentation between business units and sensitive operational systems limited the blast radius even after the development environment itself was compromised. On the response side, a DOJ-authorized disclosure delay paired with a same-day CISA Emergency Directive meant federal agencies got an actionable, dated mitigation plan the moment the breach became public, instead of a vague warning followed by a scramble.

Senior Security Advice

Turn On S3 Block Public Access for Every Bucket and the Whole Account

The Advice

Turn on all four Amazon S3 Block Public Access settings at the account level, not just bucket by bucket, so no storage bucket can be made public even by a future mistake.

Why It Matters

An S3 bucket is just a container for objects, and by default it's private - but one misconfigured bucket policy, one access control list set wrong, or one developer testing something in a hurry can flip that to public with no warning banner and no alarm. Once a bucket is public, anyone with the URL can read everything in it, and automated scanners on the internet are constantly probing for exactly this. The incident class this prevents is one of the most common cloud breaches on record: publicly exposed storage buckets leaking customer records, internal documents, or credentials, discovered not by the company but by a researcher or an attacker scanning the open internet. AWS built Block Public Access specifically because bucket-by-bucket discipline kept failing - someone, somewhere, eventually gets a setting wrong.

How to Implement It

  1. Turn on all four Block Public Access settings - block public ACLs, ignore public ACLs, block public bucket policies, and restrict public buckets - at the account level in the S3 console or via aws s3control put-public-access-block, so the setting applies no matter how a bucket gets created.
  2. For multiple AWS accounts, set Block Public Access as an AWS Organizations policy so it propagates to every member account and can't be overridden locally.
  3. Also turn on all four settings per bucket, since AWS Security Hub's Foundational Security Best Practices control S3.8 checks at that level too, and some tooling still looks there first.
  4. Verify it's actually working, not just enabled: use AWS Config or Security Hub to continuously monitor the setting, and periodically try to attach a public bucket policy in a test account to confirm it gets rejected.
  5. Check AWS Trusted Advisor or Security Hub findings for any bucket already flagged as publicly accessible before you turn the account-level block on, so you fix exposed data instead of just freezing it in place.

The Pushback You'll Hear

"We have a bucket that legitimately needs to be public, like static website hosting, so we can't just block everything." The senior response: Block Public Access settings can be applied selectively, and a handful of buckets that genuinely serve public content should be the deliberate exception, documented and reviewed, not the default. AWS's own guidance treats this as the first line of defense specifically because it assumes most buckets have no business being public, and the organization-level control still lets you carve out exceptions for the few that do. The real risk isn't blocking too much - it's leaving the door open account-wide because one bucket needed it closed.

Senior Talk Tracks

Multi-AZ Is Not Disaster Recovery

If your AWS architecture diagram says "multi-AZ" and someone on your team calls that "disaster recovery," I want you to stop them right there. Those are not the same thing, and mixing them up is one of the most common gaps I see in cloud design. Here's what you need to know.

Let's start with what multi-AZ actually means. AZ stands for Availability Zone — think of it as one data center, or a small cluster of them, inside a single AWS region. A region, like US East or Tokyo, usually contains three or more of these Availability Zones, built far enough apart that a fire or a power failure in one shouldn't take down the others. When you run "multi-AZ," you're copying your database or your servers across two or three of these zones in the same region. If one zone has a bad day, your application keeps running out of the others, usually automatically, usually in seconds.

So why isn't that disaster recovery? Because multi-AZ only protects you from a problem that stays inside one region. It does nothing if the failure is bigger than that — a region-wide networking event, a region-wide outage, or in the most severe case, the region itself becoming unreachable. Real disaster recovery means you have a second, separate copy of your system running in a completely different region, one that can take over if the first region goes dark entirely.

Here's the senior take. A junior engineer sees "multi-AZ enabled" on a database and checks the disaster recovery box. A senior engineer asks a harder question: what happens to us if the entire region fails, not just one building in it? That's a real failure mode — regions have gone down, even if rarely — and multi-AZ was never designed to answer it.

Think of it like a house with two generators in the same city. If the power company has one bad transformer, you're covered. If a hurricane takes out the whole city's grid, both generators are useless, because they were never protecting you from that scale of problem in the first place.

So next time this comes up at work, here's a good opening line: "We're multi-AZ — but what's our actual plan if the whole region goes down?"

Key Takeaways

  1. Multi-AZ protects against a failure inside one region; disaster recovery is about surviving the loss of an entire region.
  2. Availability Zones are physically separate data centers within one region, built so a local failure in one doesn't take down the others.
  3. True multi-region disaster recovery requires its own replication, failover plan, and testing — it isn't a side effect of enabling multi-AZ.

Reader Question

Q: "If multi-AZ already gives us automatic failover, why would we ever pay for a second region on top of it?"
