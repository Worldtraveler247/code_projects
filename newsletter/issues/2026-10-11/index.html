Current Events

The US Treasury Department Breach via BeyondTrust

Here's an incident where the attacker never touched the victim's own front door at all -- they walked in through a vendor's key, and the victim didn't even know that key existed until it was already used. In December 2024, China-linked hackers reached workstations inside the US Treasury Department without ever attacking Treasury directly. Here's how it actually happened.

Treasury relied on a company called BeyondTrust for privileged remote access -- the kind of tool IT support uses to remotely reach into a user's machine and fix something, or reset a password, without being physically at the desk. According to BeyondTrust's own security investigation, the company detected suspicious activity in its Remote Support SaaS service on December 2nd, 2024. What it found: an attacker had gotten hold of an API key -- a kind of digital master password one piece of software uses to talk to another -- and that key was able to reset passwords on local application accounts inside BeyondTrust's own remote-support product.

Here's the chain. That stolen API key let the attacker remotely reach a small number of BeyondTrust's customers, Treasury among them. According to Treasury's own letter to the Senate Banking Committee, the attacker used that access to reach Treasury employee workstations and view a number of unclassified documents, including systems tied to the Office of Financial Research and the Office of Foreign Assets Control -- the office that administers US sanctions. Treasury called it a "major incident" and attributed it, based on available indicators, to a China state-sponsored advanced persistent threat actor.

Root cause, in one sentence: a single compromised key for a third-party remote-support tool was powerful enough to reach into a federal agency's workstations, because the trust Treasury placed in that vendor's access path was never independently checked at Treasury's own door.

Here's what actually contained it, and this is the part worth knowing cold. BeyondTrust revoked the compromised key the same day it confirmed the compromise, and took the affected service offline for investigation. Treasury, by its own account, stopped using BeyondTrust's service entirely once notified, and worked immediately with the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency and the FBI. Weeks later, CISA stated publicly that its investigation found no evidence the compromise had reached any federal agency besides Treasury. Be honest about the limit here: "no evidence of further spread" is a statement about what investigators found, not a guarantee about what never happened, and BeyondTrust's own advisories for the underlying vulnerabilities in this product line, tracked as CVE-2024-12356 and CVE-2024-12686, came out after this specific incident, not before it -- meaning the exact technical flaw the attacker may have ridden in on wasn't something Treasury or BeyondTrust had a chance to patch ahead of time.

Here's what a senior engineer, or a senior ISSO, would change in how they work because of this. Never treat a vendor's remote-access tool as a trusted extension of your own network just because it's sold for IT support -- a RHEL 9 or AWS lab exercise worth running is putting any third-party remote-support or PAM integration behind its own monitored boundary, with alerts on anything that integration does outside its normal pattern, so a compromised vendor key shows up as an anomaly at your door, not just at theirs.

Control Failures

# Failure NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Control Family
1 A third-party vendor's remote-support API key was sufficient on its own to reach and act on Treasury workstations, with no additional Treasury-side authentication layered on top of the vendor's access path. AC-17 — Remote Access
2 The compromised API key functioned as a long-lived, powerful authenticator with no evidence of regular rotation or scoped, time-limited permissions. IA-5 — Authenticator Management
3 Treasury's reliance on an external privileged-access vendor was not matched by documented supply-chain risk controls covering what that vendor's access could reach inside Treasury's own environment. SR-3 — Supply Chain Controls and Processes
4 There was a multi-day gap between BeyondTrust first detecting anomalous activity (Dec 2) and notifying the affected customer, Treasury (Dec 8), delaying the start of Treasury's own incident response. IR-6 — Incident Reporting
5 Critical vulnerabilities in the same BeyondTrust product line (CVE-2024-12356, CVE-2024-12686) were disclosed only after this incident, reflecting a gap in proactive flaw identification for a tool with this level of privileged access. SI-2 — Flaw Remediation
6 No segmentation boundary limited a vendor remote-support integration from reaching sensitive offices by name — including the Office of Foreign Assets Control — rather than being scoped to only the specific workstations it needed. AC-6 / SC-7 — Least Privilege / Boundary Protection

What Held

  • Fast vendor-side detection and key revocation. BeyondTrust caught the anomaly on its own, inside its own service, and revoked the compromised key the same day it confirmed the compromise — the breach was found and cut off at the vendor layer, not discovered by the victim first.
  • Immediate vendor disengagement. Treasury's own account says it stopped using the compromised BeyondTrust service as soon as it was notified, removing the specific access path the attacker had used.
  • Confirmed, bounded scope. CISA stated publicly that its investigation found no evidence the compromise reached any federal agency beyond Treasury, meaning the blast radius was contained to one agency rather than spreading across BeyondTrust's federal customer base.
  • Be honest about the limit of all three. None of this explains how the API key was stolen in the first place, and "no evidence of further spread" is a statement about what investigators were able to confirm, not a claim that spread was structurally impossible. Naming that gap, instead of calling this a clean save, is the senior move.

Senior Security Advice

Throttle and Lock Out Brute-Force Login Attempts

The Advice

Throttle every login endpoint so failed password attempts get slower and eventually blocked, instead of letting an attacker guess passwords as fast as the network allows.

Why It Matters

A login form with no rate limiting is an open invitation to automated password guessing. Without a cap, an attacker's script can fire thousands of password attempts per minute against a single account, or try one common password against thousands of accounts (password spraying), and the server answers every single one instantly. Either approach eventually lands on a weak or reused password, and the attacker is in — no exploit, no malware, just raw guessing at scale. This is the mechanism behind a large share of account takeover incidents: credential-stuffing campaigns that replay breached username-password pairs across login pages until a match works, something OWASP's testing guidance treats as a baseline authentication weakness to check for on every build.

How to Implement It

  1. Rate-limit by account and by source together — capping only by IP address lets a botnet spread attempts across thousands of addresses and dodge the limit entirely.
  2. Follow NIST SP 800-63B's guidance: cap consecutive failed attempts per account well below its stated ceiling of 100, and add escalating delays (seconds, then minutes) before a hard lockout kicks in.
  3. Make lockouts temporary and time-based rather than permanent, and never reveal whether the lockout triggered because the username didn't exist or the password was wrong — that distinction itself is a hint to attackers.
  4. Layer in a CAPTCHA or equivalent friction after a handful of failures, not from the first attempt, so legitimate users barely notice it.
  5. Verify the control is live, not just configured: script a test that fires repeated bad-password attempts against a disposable test account and confirm the response actually slows or blocks after the threshold, then check authentication logs weekly for accounts with an unusual spike in failed attempts.

The Pushback You'll Hear

"Lockouts just give attackers a way to deny service to our real users." That's a fair risk, which is why the senior-engineer answer isn't a permanent lock — it's a short, escalating delay plus per-account and per-source rate limiting together. A ten-minute cooldown after repeated failures is a minor inconvenience for a real user who mistyped a password; it's a wall for a script trying thousands of guesses a minute.

Tech News

A security research firm clocked attackers probing a brand-new Atlassian flaw within two hours of a public proof-of-concept going live — not two days, two hours. If your organization runs Jira, Confluence, or Bitbucket on its own servers rather than in Atlassian's cloud, that clock is the headline today. Two stories: that Atlassian file-read flaw, and a Fortinet email-security zero-day whose federal patch deadline has already quietly come and gone.

Start with Atlassian. According to Atlassian's own advisory and reporting from BleepingComputer and eSecurity Planet, the company disclosed CVE-2026-21589 on October 5th: a critical, 9.3-severity flaw affecting eight self-hosted Data Center products, including Jira, Confluence, and Bitbucket. An unauthenticated attacker who already knows the exact file name and path can pull that file straight out of the application's web root — no login required. Atlassian's cloud products were already safe; this hits only the self-managed versions many government and defense-adjacent organizations still run. The real story is the timeline: watchTowr Labs published working proof-of-concept code on October 6th, and honeypot sensors logged a hundred and fifty-eight exploitation attempts from twenty-six different IP addresses within about a day. My read: that compressed window is the pattern now, not the exception. If your patch process still assumes you have a week of breathing room after a public disclosure, Atlassian's own numbers say you have hours.

Next up, Fortinet. CISA added CVE-2026-104286, a critical FortiMail zero-day, to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on October 1st, giving federal agencies until October 4th to patch or mitigate. According to Fortinet's own advisory and reporting from Help Net Security, the flaw lets an unauthenticated attacker write arbitrary files to the underlying system through a crafted web request, and in observed attacks it's already been used to drop backdoors and web shells. Here's the detail worth saying out loud: that federal deadline is now a week past, and Fortinet still hasn't shipped a full patch — only a workaround. My take: when a vendor can't ship a fix on the government's own emergency timeline, the workaround isn't optional homework. It's the only thing standing between your mail gateway and a web shell.

Same lesson both times today: the gap between disclosure and exploitation has shrunk to hours, and a missed deadline doesn't pause the clock — it just means you're exposed longer.
