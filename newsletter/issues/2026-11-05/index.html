Current Events

The 3CX Double Supply Chain Attack

Imagine a supply-chain attack that starts with a completely different supply-chain attack, from a completely different company, two years earlier -- and the victim in the middle never had any business relationship with where the attack actually began. That's exactly what happened to 3CX, a VoIP phone-system vendor, and Mandiant called it the first confirmed case of one software supply chain attack leading directly into a second one.

3CX makes a desktop softphone app used by call centers and businesses worldwide. According to Mandiant's own investigation, commissioned by 3CX, and corroborated by CrowdStrike, attackers trojanized 3CX's official Windows and Mac desktop app and pushed it out through 3CX's own legitimate update mechanism in March of 2023 -- meaning customers who updated normally were installing malware straight from the vendor's own servers.

Here's the chain, and this is the part that makes it remarkable. Mandiant traced the intrusion back to a 3CX employee's personal computer, which had a completely unrelated piece of software installed -- a financial trading application called X_TRADER, made by a company called Trading Technologies. X_TRADER had itself been trojanized by the same attackers, using a stolen code-signing certificate, sometime in 2021, and it sat on Trading Technologies' own download page for nearly a year before anyone noticed. The 3CX employee had downloaded that already-compromised software for personal use, on a machine that also had access to 3CX's build environment. One infected personal download, on one employee's machine, became the entry point into an entire vendor's official software supply chain.

Root cause, in one sentence: trust flowed in two directions that nobody had mapped -- a personal download on an employee's machine carried enough access into the corporate build pipeline that a completely unrelated company's compromise became 3CX's compromise too.

What actually held: once CrowdStrike and other security vendors flagged the malicious 3CX update in late March 2023, 3CX pulled the compromised installers and shipped clean versions within days, and Mandiant's resulting investigation -- which is the reason any of this double-attack chain is even known -- produced one of the most detailed public breakdowns of a software supply-chain compromise to date. Multiple independent security vendors corroborated the finding quickly, which is what turned a confusing, hard-to-explain intrusion into a credible, well-documented incident instead of a disputed rumor.

So what does this mean for how a senior engineer thinks about personal devices and trust boundaries? A senior engineer treats a build environment's trust boundary as including every device with access to it -- corporate-issued or not -- because this incident shows a personal download, with zero connection to the employer, was enough to reach a production build pipeline. On a RHEL or Podman lab, that means practicing exactly that: separating any machine or account with build or signing access from general-purpose personal use, instead of assuming "it's just my personal laptop" means it's outside the blast radius.

Control Failures

# Failure NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Control Family
1 A personal device used for unrelated software had enough access into the corporate build environment to let a personal-use compromise become a supply-chain compromise. AC-20 — Use of External Systems
2 3CX's build and release pipeline had no control preventing a single compromised endpoint from reaching production installer builds. SC-7 — Boundary Protection
3 Trading Technologies' own code-signing certificate was usable by attackers to sign malicious installers, with no detection of the unauthorized signing activity. IA-5 — Authenticator Management
4 The trojanized X_TRADER installer remained downloadable from Trading Technologies' own site for nearly a year with no integrity monitoring catching the substitution. SI-7 — Software, Firmware, and Information Integrity
5 3CX's own automatic update mechanism distributed the trojanized app to customers with no anomaly detection on the build or release process itself. CM-3 — Configuration Change Control

What Held

  • Fast detection once the malicious update shipped. Multiple independent security vendors (Todyl, CrowdStrike, SentinelOne) flagged the trojanized 3CX update within days of it going out, not weeks.
  • A thorough, transparent investigation. 3CX commissioned Mandiant's investigation rather than managing the incident quietly, and Mandiant's willingness to keep digging is exactly what uncovered the earlier Trading Technologies compromise — most incidents stop at the first answer that explains the symptoms.
  • Rapid remediation. 3CX pulled the compromised installers and shipped clean versions within days of confirmation, limiting how long new infections could occur.

Senior Security Advice

Maintain and Test Break-Glass Emergency Access Accounts

The Advice

Keep two dedicated, cloud-only emergency access accounts with standing admin rights, exclude them from the login rules that could lock everyone out, and actually test them on a schedule — don't just create them and forget them.

Why It Matters

Every identity system eventually has a bad day: a multi-factor authentication service outage, an identity provider that goes down, or a conditional access policy that gets pushed with a mistake in it and blocks every admin from signing in. If the only way into your tenant is through the same login path that just broke, nobody can fix it. "Break-glass" accounts exist for exactly that scenario — a credential that doesn't depend on the system that failed.

The incident class this guards against is the total admin lockout: an organization's own security controls work so well that they lock out the people who need to fix them. Microsoft's own guidance on managing emergency access accounts in Microsoft Entra ID is built around this failure mode, which is why it treats these two accounts as a deliberate, monitored exception to normal access rules rather than a convenience.

How to Implement It

  1. Create exactly two emergency access accounts that are cloud-only — not synced from on-premises Active Directory and not federated through any external identity provider, so they don't inherit the outage that break-glass is supposed to survive.
  2. Assign them the Global Administrator role permanently (not through an eligible/just-in-time assignment like Privileged Identity Management), since the whole point is that they work even if the approval workflow itself is down.
  3. Exclude them from any conditional access policy that blocks or restricts sign-in, while keeping them inside report-only policies so you still get visibility into what they would have triggered.
  4. Route their sign-in and audit logs into continuous monitoring and alert on every single use — a break-glass sign-in should always page someone, because there's no routine reason for one to happen.
  5. Verify it's actually working: on a fixed schedule (Microsoft points to roughly every 90 days), log in with each account for real, confirm the credential still works and still has Global Administrator rights, and confirm the alert actually fires when you do it.

The Pushback You'll Hear

"Standing Global Administrator access on two accounts violates least privilege — everything should be just-in-time." The rebuttal: least privilege governs everyday admin work, and that's exactly what just-in-time, approval-based access is for. Break-glass accounts are the deliberate exception that exists for the moment the just-in-time system itself is unreachable. The trade isn't "standing access versus least privilege" — it's "two tightly monitored accounts with permanent access versus zero way into your own tenant during an outage." Heavy monitoring is what makes that trade acceptable, not an afterthought bolted onto it.

Tech News

If your company runs WordPress, CISA just confirmed attackers are already exploiting a flaw in it that was patched less than a week ago — and if you run Linux servers, a security research team just showed that an AI model can write a working, unauthenticated root exploit for a bug that's been sitting in the kernel since 2016. Two stories tonight: a WordPress core vulnerability now on CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities list with a federal deadline this week, and a Linux kernel flaw whose real headline isn't the bug itself — it's who wrote the exploit for it.

Start with WordPress. According to CISA's own Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, corroborated by BleepingComputer's reporting, the agency added CVE-2026-119977 on November 3rd after confirming active exploitation. WordPress's own advisory describes the flaw as a weakness in the platform's upgrade process that can lead to remote code execution during the upgrade window itself — the moment a site is pulling down and applying a new version is the moment it's exposed. WordPress shipped a fix roughly a week before CISA's confirmation, and federal civilian agencies have until November 6th to patch. Here's my read: a flaw that lives inside the update mechanism is a nasty kind of irony — the safest thing you can normally do, keeping software current, is briefly the most dangerous thing, and that's exactly the kind of edge case patch management has to account for, not just patch cadence.

Next up, turning to the kernel. According to independent technical write-ups corroborating Anthropic's own October 27th research disclosure, a security team used an internal AI research model to develop a working exploit for CVE-2026-98432, a use-after-free bug in the Linux kernel's IPv4 TCP options handling — a flaw that traces back to a 2016 kernel commit. The proof of concept lets an attacker with no credentials at all execute commands as root on a server, provided that server accepts multipath TCP, or MPTCP, connections. As of late October, researchers report no sign of this being exploited in the wild outside that research exercise, and the kernel's own severity rating sits at a maximum 9.8, though Red Hat's own draft score is more conservative. Here's my take: don't let the words "MPTCP" and "uncommon configuration" talk you into skipping this one — the underlying use-after-free sits in general IPv4 TCP code, not just the MPTCP path, so treating this as a kernel-wide patch priority, not a niche one, is the senior call here.

Tonight's thread: one of these flaws is already being used against real victims, the other is a warning shot from a lab — and the discipline that matters is treating both as this week's work, not next month's backlog.
