Current Events

The kCTF Container Escape (CVE-2022-0185)

A Linux kernel bug that earned a group of researchers thirty-one thousand, three hundred and thirty-seven dollars in 2022 turned out, two and a half years later, to be worth something very different to real attackers: a proven way out of a locked-down container. Today: CVE-2022-0185, the kernel flaw a CTF team used to escape Google's own hardened Kubernetes test environment -- and that the U.S. government now confirms is being used in real attacks.

Here's how it actually happened. In January 2022, a team of security researchers competing together as "Crusaders of Rust" were fuzzing the Linux kernel -- throwing huge volumes of malformed input at it to see what broke. What they found was a bug in a function called legacy_parse_param, inside the kernel's filesystem-context code, the part that parses options when a filesystem gets mounted. According to the official CVE record, the function has an integer underflow: a length calculation that can wrap around into a huge number instead of going negative, and that wraparound lets an attacker overflow a heap buffer with data they control.

On an ordinary Linux machine, that's already a privilege-escalation bug -- a regular user can ride it to root. The researchers didn't stop there. They rebuilt their exploit specifically to break out of Google's kCTF environment, Google's own hardened Kubernetes container infrastructure, built to be about as locked-down as a container gets. And it worked. According to the team's own published writeup, they escaped the container, rooted the underlying host, and Google paid out a bug bounty of $31,337 through its container-escape rewards program.

Root cause, in one sentence: a two-and-a-half-year-old integer underflow in a rarely audited corner of the kernel's filesystem code let a process step straight through the namespace boundary that every container on that host was depending on to stay contained.

Here's the senior-level detail on what actually held. Exploiting this bug required one specific Linux capability, CAP_SYS_ADMIN. If a pod or container was never granted that capability, this bug was effectively unreachable from inside it, patched or not. That's the real-world case for least privilege at the capability level, not just the user account level. And the turnaround was fast: once reported, a fix landed upstream within about two weeks, and major distributions shipped patched kernels right behind it.

So why bring this up today instead of treating it as old news? Because in August of 2024, two and a half years after that bounty was paid, CISA added CVE-2022-0185 to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog -- meaning real attackers, not bounty hunters, are now confirmed to be using it against systems that are still unpatched.

So what would a senior engineer flag here? A senior engineer doesn't treat "patched years ago" as a reason to stop checking for a vulnerability -- a KEV listing that old is proof that unpatched kernels have a very long shelf life in production, and auditing who actually holds CAP_SYS_ADMIN matters just as much as tracking patch level. On a Kubernetes or Podman lab, that's a concrete, checkable habit: auditing which workloads actually need elevated capabilities, and dropping every one that doesn't.

What Held

  • Capability-gating limited exposure. Workloads that were never granted CAP_SYS_ADMIN were not exploitable by this bug, even unpatched — a real demonstration of least privilege acting as a backstop against a kernel 0-day nobody could patch in time.
  • A fast, functioning patch pipeline. Once reported, kernel maintainers shipped an upstream fix within about two weeks, and major distributions followed close behind.
  • A legitimate bug-bounty channel. Google's kCTF vulnerability rewards program gave researchers an incentive to report the bug and get paid for it instead of selling it or sitting on it — a large part of why there was roughly a two-year head start before CISA's KEV listing confirmed real attackers were using it.

Senior Security Advice

Isolate Privileged Access on Dedicated, Hardened Admin Workstations

The Advice

Give every administrator a separate, hardened workstation used only for privileged tasks, and block privileged logins from any other device.

Why It Matters

The device an administrator uses for everyday work — checking email, browsing the web, opening attachments — is also the device most exposed to phishing and malware. If that same device is ever used to authenticate as a domain admin or cloud global admin, a single infection hands the attacker those credentials too. From there, the attacker doesn't need to break anything else: they already have the keys.

This is the exact pivot behind a huge share of ransomware and large-scale breach incidents. An attacker compromises one ordinary endpoint through phishing, finds cached or re-used privileged credentials on it, and escalates straight to domain-wide or tenant-wide control within hours. Microsoft's own Zero Trust guidance states plainly that a single compromised administrative workstation is enough for an attacker to reach identity systems and enterprise-wide administration. Separating "the device I browse the internet on" from "the device I administer the network from" closes that pivot.

How to Implement It

  1. Identify which roles actually hold Tier 0 power — domain admins, cloud global/tenant admins, identity-provider admins, CI/CD release managers with production push rights — and scope the control to them first, not the whole company.
  2. Provision each of those administrators a dedicated, locked-down device (or a strictly isolated virtual machine) built with no general web browsing, no email client, no everyday productivity apps, and endpoint detection enabled.
  3. Set identity policy — conditional access rules, device compliance tags — so privileged accounts can only authenticate for privileged roles from a device recognized as an admin workstation, and require strong authentication to even join a device to that group.
  4. Restrict who can provision or approve a new admin workstation, so the control itself can't be quietly bypassed.
  5. Verify it's actually enforced: regularly check identity sign-in logs for blocked privileged-login attempts from non-admin devices, scan the admin workstations themselves for configuration drift or vulnerabilities, and periodically test a privileged sign-in from an ordinary laptop to confirm it gets denied rather than just assuming the policy holds.

The Pushback You'll Hear

"Admins already juggle enough devices — this doubles their hardware and our overhead." It's a real cost, but it's small next to the alternative: one phished laptop turning into a domain-wide ransomware event. The fix doesn't have to mean a second laptop for everyone — an isolated virtual machine used only for privileged sessions satisfies the same separation at a fraction of the cost, and scoping the requirement to true Tier 0 roles keeps the overhead proportional to the risk it removes.

Tech News

If you manage a SonicWall firewall with SSL VPN turned on, there's an active attack you need to know about tonight: attackers are getting past multi-factor authentication entirely, and they're doing it with credentials that are perfectly valid. CISA confirmed this is already happening in the real world, not in a lab. One story tonight, but it's a dense one, so let's get into the mechanism — because it's a lesson that applies far beyond one firewall vendor.

Here's what happened. SonicWall disclosed CVE-2026-19868 in its own security advisory, covering SSL VPN services on its SonicOS firewalls — the Gen 7 and Gen 8 lines a lot of mid-size companies use to let remote employees connect into the network. The flaw is what's tracked as a single-factor authentication weakness. In plain terms: if that SSL VPN is configured to require SAML — Security Assertion Markup Language, the standard that lets someone log in once through an identity provider and have that trust extend to other services — an attacker who holds valid credentials for a different, alternate login method on that same box can use those instead, and walk straight past the SAML check. Here's the part that should get your attention: a lot of organizations enforce their multi-factor authentication through that SAML flow. So bypassing SAML quietly bypasses the MFA sitting behind it too.

CISA added this to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on November 6th, citing evidence of active exploitation, and gave federal civilian agencies until November 20th to patch. SonicWall has shipped fixed firmware — SonicOS 7.3.4-7013 for Gen 7 devices, 8.2.3-8015 for Gen 8 — and says there's no workaround short of updating.

So what's the senior-level read here? A security engineer doesn't treat multi-factor authentication as one control — they treat it as a chain, and a chain is only as strong as every link an attacker is allowed to pick from. If a system offers two different paths to authenticate and only one of them is wired to MFA, that second path isn't a harmless legacy option — it's an open side door. The move a senior engineer makes when auditing this kind of appliance is checking every enabled authentication method, not just the one that's supposed to be mandatory, and switching off anything that isn't actually needed.

The through-line tonight: multi-factor authentication only protects the door you're actually watching.

Patch any SonicWall Gen 7 or Gen 8 firewall to the fixed builds, and go check which login methods are quietly still enabled on every SAML-protected system you run.
