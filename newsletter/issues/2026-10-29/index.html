Current Events

The Accenture Cloud Leak

Four unlocked storage buckets. No password required. Inside one of them: the master key to a global consulting firm's own cloud encryption system, sitting in a plain-text file. Here's how one of the biggest technology consulting companies on earth nearly handed a stranger the keys to its own kingdom.

In September 2017, researchers at the cyber-risk firm UpGuard were running routine internet-wide scans for exposed cloud storage when they found four Amazon S3 buckets belonging to Accenture, configured for public access -- meaning anyone who found the web address could download what was inside, no account and no password needed. According to UpGuard's own published findings, corroborated by Help Net Security, SecurityWeek, and Threatpost, those buckets held a startling amount of Accenture's own internal infrastructure: authentication credentials, decryption keys, and certificate information tied to the enterprise cloud platform Accenture uses to run cloud services for its own clients.

Here's the detail worth saying slowly. One of those buckets held what researchers described as a master access key for Accenture's own AWS Key Management Service account, stored in an unencrypted, plain-text file. Whoever held that key could potentially have decrypted other secured data across parts of Accenture's cloud environment. Other exposed files reportedly included credentials tied to Accenture's Google and Azure accounts, plus VPN keys described as used in production.

Root cause, in one sentence: nobody required a password on four Amazon S3 buckets that weren't holding ordinary business files -- they were holding the literal keys to the business's own cloud infrastructure.

What actually limited the damage, and this is the senior-level detail worth naming: Accenture secured the exposed buckets within roughly two days of UpGuard's notification, and multiple outlets reported no evidence the exposure had been found or exploited by a malicious actor before it was closed. The control that actually worked here wasn't prevention -- it was a fast, competent response once an outside party found the hole first, and a researcher who disclosed privately before publishing.

So what does that mean for the kind of environment a senior engineer is responsible for? A senior engineer treats "is this bucket actually private" as something to verify continuously, not a setting you check once at creation -- because this incident shows the most dangerous exposure usually isn't customer records sitting at the edge of the business, it's the infrastructure credentials that unlock everything else behind them. On an AWS lab, that means practicing exactly that: confirming S3 Block Public Access is enforced account-wide, not assuming a bucket is private just because nobody meant to make it public.

What Held

  • Remediation was fast once the problem was found. Accenture secured all four buckets within roughly two days of UpGuard's notification — a senior-level point worth naming out loud, because the control that actually limited this incident wasn't prevention, it was response speed.
  • Responsible disclosure gave Accenture a head start. UpGuard notified Accenture privately and held publication until after the exposure was closed, which is exactly the kind of researcher relationship that turns a potential breach into a near-miss instead of a live incident.
  • No confirmed exploitation. Multiple outlets reported no evidence the exposed data was accessed or misused by a malicious party before the buckets were secured — a reminder that "exposed" and "breached" aren't automatically the same thing, though it's also a reminder you can only ever say that with the confidence an outside researcher's timeline allows.

Senior Security Advice

Require Mutual, Multi-Factor Wi-Fi Authentication Instead of Shared Passphrases

The Advice

Kill the shared Wi-Fi passphrase on every corporate network, and replace it with 802.1X authentication that verifies each device and user individually.

Why It Matters

A shared Wi-Fi passphrase — one password every laptop on the network types in — has no concept of identity. Everyone who has ever worked there, every contractor, every guest who glanced at a sticky note on a monitor, knows it, and there is no way to revoke access from one of those people without rekeying every single device in the building. That password also has to survive being captured over the air: WPA2 and WPA3-Personal both authenticate with a handshake that an attacker can capture from a parking lot and crack offline, at their own pace, with no connection to the target network at all. The 2017 KRACK vulnerability showed how fragile even the WPA2 protocol itself could be once attackers had something to target. Once someone gets that passphrase, by cracking it or by someone just handing it over, they are inside your internal network — past the firewall, sitting next to your printers, your IoT devices, and anything else that trusted "being on the Wi-Fi" as proof of belonging there. Enterprise-grade wireless authentication closes that gap by checking who is connecting, not just what they typed.

How to Implement It

  1. Move every corporate SSID to WPA2-Enterprise or WPA3-Enterprise using 802.1X with EAP-TLS, authenticating against a RADIUS server tied to your identity provider and certificate authority — not a pre-shared key, on any network that isn't an isolated guest VLAN.
  2. Issue per-user or per-device certificates through your PKI or MDM so a single person's or device's network access can be revoked on its own, instead of forcing a passphrase rotation across the entire fleet.
  3. Deploy rogue access point and rogue client detection, which CIS Controls v7.1 (Control 15.2) and CISA's own Wi-Fi security guide both call out as a required capability for catching an unauthorized AP spoofing your SSID.
  4. Verify it's actually working, not just configured: pull the RADIUS/AAA authentication logs and confirm every successful association maps to an identifiable certificate or user rather than a blanket shared secret, and test that an unauthorized device trying to join gets flagged by your rogue-detection alerting, not silently ignored.
  5. Treat Wi-Fi clients the same as wired clients once they're on: put them behind the same network access control and segmentation policy, so getting onto the Wi-Fi is never treated as equivalent to being a trusted device on the internal network.

The Pushback You'll Hear

"Standing up RADIUS and certificates for Wi-Fi is way more work than typing a passphrase into a router." The senior-engineer rebuttal: that setup cost is paid once, and most identity providers and MDM platforms now offer managed RADIUS specifically to remove that excuse. Compare it to the real, recurring cost of the alternative — every time someone leaves the company, every device in the building needs the new passphrase, and until that rotation happens, a former employee's phone is still a valid key to your network. One upfront integration beats an indefinite, repeating fire drill.

Tech News

A firewall feature that was supposed to stop unauthorized access is the reason two separate ransomware crews walked straight past multi-factor authentication this year. If your organization runs a VPN gateway or a self-hosted collaboration server, tonight's two stories are for you. First: ransomware operators using a patched VPN flaw to skip MFA entirely and pivot into your virtualization layer. Then: a self-hosted office-suite add-on under active attack right now.

Start with the VPN story. According to Palo Alto Networks' own Unit 42 threat research, corroborated by BleepingComputer and HackRead, two ransomware operations — known as Qilin and INC — have been exploiting an authentication-bypass flaw in the GlobalProtect portal and gateway since around June. The flaw, tracked as CVE-2026-0257, lets an attacker open a VPN session without ever supplying valid credentials, which means multi-factor authentication never even gets a chance to fire. Palo Alto Networks patched it back in May. Here's what's new: Unit 42's latest update says the real story now isn't the initial bypass, it's what happens after — attackers pivot from the VPN gateway straight into the virtualization layer, pulling domain credentials directly off VMware virtual disks, then using those stolen credentials to move through the rest of the network. My read: patching the VPN gateway doesn't close this exposure by itself, because the attacker's actual target is one layer deeper, in virtualization infrastructure that often has far broader reach than anyone's audited.

Next up — turning to self-hosted collaboration software. According to CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, corroborated by BleepingComputer and Security Affairs, attackers have been planting PHP web shells on Nextcloud servers since September 10th by exploiting an unauthenticated command-injection flaw in Collabora Online's Built-in CODE Server add-on. CISA added the flaw, CVE-2026-110447, to its catalog on October 23rd. Collabora's own advisory is blunt about the fix: disabling the add-on isn't enough, vulnerable installs have to be upgraded to version 26.4.303 or removed entirely. My take: the component that earns a CVE isn't always the core application people think to patch — this exposure sits in an optional add-on bundled for convenience, which is exactly the kind of piece that falls outside most organizations' patch inventory.

Tonight's thread: the credential you protect at the front door and the add-on you forgot you installed can hand an attacker the exact same result — a foothold nobody was watching.
