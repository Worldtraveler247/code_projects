Current Events

The ChaosDB Azure Cosmos DB Exposure

In February of 2021, Microsoft quietly turned on a new feature for every Azure Cosmos database on the planet. For about six months, that one feature meant a stranger with zero credentials of their own could potentially read, write, and delete your production database. No phishing email, no malware -- just a notebook feature nobody asked to have switched on. Today: ChaosDB, the Azure Cosmos DB flaw Wiz Research chained together, and the shared management plane it exposed.

Here's how it actually happened. According to Wiz's own research writeup and Microsoft's own security response blog, Cosmos DB ships an optional Jupyter Notebook feature for querying your data, and in February 2021 Microsoft turned it on by default for every account. Each notebook runs inside its own container, supposedly sandboxed to its own customer. On August 9th, 2021, Wiz researchers found they could escalate privileges inside that container and route around a firewall rule meant to keep the sandbox shut, and escape the notebook container entirely.

From there the chain got worse. Once outside the sandbox, the researchers reached Azure's internal WireServer service and pulled real certificates off of it, including a wildcard certificate for the notebook service itself. Those certificates let them authenticate directly to the regional Service Fabric cluster that manages the notebook feature for every customer sharing that cluster. From inside that shared management plane, they could pull the plaintext Primary Key for any Cosmos DB account running on it -- no authentication of their own required beyond the certificates they'd already lifted.

Root cause, in one sentence: a default-on feature's sandbox could be escaped into a shared, multi-tenant management plane, and that plane handed out full administrative keys to every tenant on it without re-checking who was actually asking.

Here's what actually held. Wiz reported this through coordinated disclosure instead of publishing it immediately, and Microsoft disabled the notebook feature across all of Cosmos DB within about forty-eight hours of that report. Microsoft says it found no evidence of exploitation beyond the researchers' own testing, and it paid out a forty-thousand-dollar bounty for the report. Where it didn't hold cleanly: Microsoft could not rotate the exposed keys itself, so it had to email customers directly and ask them to regenerate their own primary keys -- and Wiz has argued every customer running the feature should have assumed exposure, since the hole had likely existed since that February rollout.

So what would a senior engineer take from this one? A senior engineer never assumes a managed cloud service's internal sandbox is the only boundary protecting their data -- your own access model should assume a provider's shared control plane can fail, and should limit what a single leaked key can actually reach. On an AWS or Kubernetes lab, that's a concrete habit: auditing which of your own credentials are long-lived, provider-issued static keys versus short-lived, automatically- expiring tokens, and moving anything you can off a static primary key and onto scoped credentials whose rotation you control.

What Held

  • Coordinated disclosure. Wiz reported the chain to Microsoft privately and held back technical detail at Microsoft's request instead of publishing a working exploit immediately, buying time to close the hole before it was public knowledge.
  • A fast kill switch. Microsoft disabled the vulnerable notebook feature platform-wide within about 48 hours of receiving the report — the single fastest lever available once the flaw was known, and one that didn't require patching millions of individual databases.
  • A functioning bug-bounty channel. Microsoft's $40,000 payout gave Wiz a legitimate, incentivized path to report the flaw rather than sell it, and the two companies' public statements (even where they disagree on scope) came from that same disclosure relationship.

Senior Security Advice

Patch Every CISA Known Exploited Vulnerability by Its Due Date

The Advice

Patch every vulnerability on CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog by its assigned due date, ahead of anything your patch queue is ranking purely by CVSS severity score.

Why It Matters

Most patch queues get sorted by severity score alone: fix the "critical" 9.8 before the "medium" 6.1, regardless of what's actually happening in the wild. That sorting misses the one signal that matters most to an attacker's actual playbook — whether a vulnerability is already being exploited. A medium-severity bug under active exploitation right now is a bigger real-world risk than a higher-scored bug sitting untouched on a segmented internal system with no known exploit.

That gap is exactly what mass-exploitation campaigns run on: a vulnerability gets weaponized, scanners sweep the entire internet for it within days, and organizations that were still working through a severity-only backlog get hit months after a patch was already available. It's a recurring incident class — not a single breach, but a pattern that repeats every time a widely deployed product discloses a flaw that attackers adopt faster than defenders patch it. CISA built its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalog specifically to interrupt that pattern, by naming the exact vulnerabilities with confirmed real-world exploitation and assigning each one a remediation due date.

How to Implement It

  1. Feed the KEV catalog into your vulnerability management or asset inventory tool so any asset matching a listed CVE is flagged automatically, instead of relying on someone to check the list by hand.
  2. Treat the catalog's due dates as the floor, not a suggestion. CISA's Binding Operational Directive 22-01 assigns each entry its own deadline — some as short as two weeks, others out to six months — so pull the live due date for each CVE rather than assuming one blanket SLA, and mirror or beat it even though the directive itself only legally binds federal civilian agencies.
  3. For any KEV-listed vulnerability that can't be patched immediately, put it through a formal risk-acceptance process with a compensating control — network isolation, a WAF rule, disabling the affected feature — and a hard expiration date on that exception, not an open-ended waiver.
  4. Verify the patch actually landed, not just that a ticket closed: re-scan the asset against the specific CVE after the deployment window and confirm the fixed version is what's actually running.
  5. Report KEV-specific remediation metrics separately from your general patch SLA — percent remediated within deadline, oldest open KEV exposure — up to engineering leadership, since this is the subset of vulnerabilities attackers are already using.

The Pushback You'll Hear

"We already patch by CVSS severity, so this is redundant." A severity score is a theoretical estimate of how bad a flaw could be; the KEV catalog is evidence of what attackers are actually doing with it right now. Those are different signals, and they frequently disagree — plenty of KEV entries carry moderate CVSS scores precisely because the catalog's bar is confirmed in-the-wild exploitation, not worst-case impact. A queue sorted on severity alone can leave an actively-exploited vulnerability sitting behind a theoretically-worse one that nobody has bothered to target.

Tech News

A security research team just caught attackers getting full, unauthenticated root access on a VPN appliance that had already been patched — twice. That matters because it proves a single patch almost never closes every door a vendor opens by accident. Tonight: how three separate flaws in one VPN product added up to a total compromise, and a critical, no-login-required Linux kernel bug you need to check against every server you run.

Start with Ivanti Connect Secure, the VPN — Virtual Private Network — appliance a lot of mid-size companies and government networks use for remote access. According to Volexity's incident report and Ivanti's own advisories, a threat actor researchers track as UTA0576 chained three separate vulnerabilities, tracked under the Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures system as CVE-2026-104935, CVE-2026-111357, and CVE-2026-118527, to reach unauthenticated, root-level code execution on a device already running Ivanti's current firmware. Ivanti fixed the first flaw on October 13th, and CISA, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, added it to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog that same day. Then Ivanti found the other two flaws — the ones that actually finished the job — and patched those on October 29th; CISA added both the next day. One federal deadline has already passed; the second lands November 13th, just three days from now. Here's my read: if you patched on October 13th and called it done, you were still exposed for two more weeks. A senior engineer doesn't close the ticket when a vendor ships one CVE — they close it when the vendor confirms nothing else is left in the chain.

Next up, turning to Linux itself. Red Hat, backed by independent research from Akamai and an advisory from Sophos, patched CVE-2026-98432 on November 1st — a critical use-after-free in how the Linux kernel handles incoming Transmission Control Protocol, or TCP, connections, scored 9.8 out of 10 on the Common Vulnerability Scoring System. It needs no login at all, just a crafted network connection. Red Hat fixed RHEL — Red Hat Enterprise Linux — versions 8, 9, and 10; Debian, Oracle Linux, and Amazon Linux followed. Ubuntu hasn't: as of this recording, Canonical's fix is sitting in a testing channel, not yet a standard update. My take: don't wait for every downstream distro to catch up — patch what's patched, and mitigate what isn't, today.

So here's the through-line: a patch closes the one door it was built for — it's on you to go check every other door the same bug might have opened.

If you run Ivanti Connect Secure, confirm you're on 25.1.1.3 or 22.8R2.6, not whatever version you patched to two weeks ago. If you run Linux anywhere network-facing, check it against CVE-2026-98432 today.
